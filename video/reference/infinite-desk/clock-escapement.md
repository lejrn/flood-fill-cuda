# Pendulum escapement: stop the weight falling all at once, then let a pendulum meter it out

**Chain at a glance:** Free-falling weight turns gear -> Latch locks and releases -> Pendulum works two latches

**The simple problem:** A hanging weight can turn a gear, but clock hands need it to turn slowly and steadily, not in one rush. [00:00]

## The ladder

### 1. Weight on a rope around the axle [00:00]
- **Idea:** Wind a rope around the gear's axle, hang a heavy weight from it, let it drop.
- **Why it works:** Gravity pulls the rope, which unwinds and spins the axle.
- **But:** The weight falls too fast and the gear stops almost immediately [00:04]. - `control`
- **So:** Lock the gear with a latch and release it at regular intervals [00:15]. - `periodic-release`
- **Shown as:** An iron frame with a brass gear on a small wooden stand; the weight drops and lands on the floor of the stand, and the gear stalls.
![Weight hits bottom](frames/clock-escapement/f003_0-00-08.jpg)

### 2. A latch on the gear teeth [00:15]
- **Idea:** A latch catches a tooth and holds the gear; lifting it lets the gear advance.
- **Why it works:** The weight's energy is spent one tooth at a time instead of all at once.
- **But:** Something has to move the latch at regular intervals: "the problem was how to move that latch" [00:19]. - `incomplete`
- **So:** Let a pendulum, which swings back and forth at regular intervals by itself, work the latch [00:21]. - `automate`
- **Shown as:** Close-up of a hook-shaped latch over the teeth, the engineer's face blurred behind the gear (the on-screen caption reads "lock the gear"; the transcript's "wind" is a mishearing).
![Latch above gear teeth](frames/clock-escapement/f005_0-00-18.jpg)

### Final: Pendulum with two alternating latches [00:26]
- **Idea:** The pendulum (first shown alone [00:21]) is linked to an arched piece carrying two latches that take turns catching and releasing the teeth, so the gear moves in steady, regular steps [00:26].
- **Why it solves the chain:** The weight still supplies the power (rung 1), the latch still meters it (rung 2), and the pendulum's fixed swing supplies the timing that nobody has to provide by hand.
- **Reveal:** The word "clock" arrives only in the last line, "allowed the clock's hands to move" [00:38], over a cut to a brick clock tower. "Escapement" is never named.
![Two latches over the gear](frames/clock-escapement/f008_0-00-30.jpg)

## Accuracy notes
- History is reversed: latch-type escapements (verge and foliot) ran tower clocks from about the 14th century; the pendulum came only with Huygens (1656), and the two-pallet anchor shown here around 1670.
- Omitted: each released tooth also gives the pendulum a small push, which keeps it swinging against friction; and a gear train links the escape wheel to the hands.
