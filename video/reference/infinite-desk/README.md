# The Evolution Method: how to explain any invention as a chain of simple fixes

Top-level analysis of 21 Infinite Desk shorts. Each video has its own micro-level breakdown in this folder (linked in the table in section 2). This file pulls out the patterns they share and turns them into a method you can apply to any other invention.

---

## 1. The method on one screen

1. **Name the job, not the product.** Verb + object: "heat a pot", "keep a nut from turning", "span a stream".
2. **Start at the dumbest thing that works a little.** A caveman version, a toy experiment, an everyday analogy, or a wrong intuition. It must fit on a desk and fail visibly within seconds.
3. **Ask "But?"** Find the single most obvious thing wrong with it. Make it a visible event, not a sentence.
4. **Answer "So…"** with one change: swap the material, change the shape, add a guide, turn a dial, close a loop, make the problem do the work.
5. **Repeat** until the design matches the real product. 3 to 7 rungs, about 13 seconds each.
6. **Check the scar map.** Every part of the final design should exist because of one failure you showed. If a part has no failure, add a rung or leave the part out.
7. **Reveal the name last, or never.** End on the pain that is gone ("No shaking required", "A century of dripping finally stopped").

### 2.4 The anatomy of one rung

Every rung in every video has the same four beats:

```
DESIGN        what they build or try
WHY IT WORKS  the principle, in one plain sentence
BUT           one flaw, shown as a visible event
SO            one change, which becomes the next DESIGN
```

The connecting words almost never change: **"What happens if…"** (hook) → **"So what if…"** → **"But there was a problem."** → **"So this time…"** → **"Then a genius engineer…"** (the big jump) → **"Finally…"** → **"This is the X."** / **"And that's how X was born."**

Two extra devices appear in the strongest videos:
- **"That's because…"** after a failure, so the flaw teaches the principle ([Microwave](microwave-oven.md): the vibrating plate failed *because* the molecules moved together and never collided).
- **A reframing line** halfway through: "The problem was never the pillar, it was the mud underneath" ([Underwater pillars](underwater-cofferdam.md)), or "the biggest problem was still there" ([Ondol](heated-floor-ondol.md)). It restarts the viewer's attention.

## 3. What goes wrong next: the "But" catalog

All but one of the 86 flaws fall into one of 12 types (the exception: contact horns only fire on a direct hit, a "range" problem). The counts show which types the videos lean on.

| Problem type | Count | Question that finds it | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Too weak or slow** | 17 | Is it strong, fast, hot or cold enough? | evaporating water stops cooling before ice; foot power cannot stop a fast car; slow powder gives too little thrust |
| **Control** | 11 | Does it stay put, stay aimed, stay even, release at the right moment? | barrel drifts the wrong way; rock tumbles off the pile; weight falls in one rush; wheels lock and skid; floor hot near fire, cold far away |
| **Wear or leak** | 10 | Does it last? | washer crushed and leaking; rivets loosen with vibration; wood rots; timber walls warp and gap |
| **Incomplete** | 9 | What is still missing for the job? | (analogy ladders) pattern printed but no current can flow yet; one layer is not enough |
| **Side effect** | 8 | Does it harm something else: noise, heat, smoke, drag, sway? | 60 Hz hum; smoke fills the house; drum brakes overheat; blunt nose has huge drag |
| **Doesn't work** | 6 | Does the principle work at all? | a still magnet heats nothing; pillar sinks sideways into mud; pointed shell ricochets |
| **Doesn't scale** | 6 | Does it still work bigger, wider, deeper, longer? | stepped stones collapse over wide gaps; steel plates buckle in deep water; long flues push smoke back |
| **Consumable / waste** | 6 | Does something get used up or escape? | sprayed refrigerant flies away; balls fall out of the nut; steam leaves with energy left |
| **Manual effort** | 5 | Does a person have to keep doing it or wait for it? | shaking the magnet by hand; someone waiting by the wire for a ship; hauling the rock up |
| **Baggage** | 3 | Does it need bulky extra equipment? | boiler and water tank; an igniter strapped on; a boiler in every building |
| **Safety** | 2 | Can it hurt someone? | rope snaps; welding voltage can kill |
| **Adversary** | 2 | Does someone fight back? | warships demagnetise their hulls; fake engine sounds fool the mine |

**The question ladder.** In the videos the flaws tend to come in a rough order. Use it as a default sequence of questions to generate the next "But":

1. Does it work at all?
2. Does it stay where it should?
3. Does it work without a person doing it?
4. Is it strong / fast enough?
5. Does it waste something, or drag along extra equipment?
6. Does it cause a side effect?
7. Does it last?
8. Can it be controlled precisely and safely?
9. Does it still work bigger, deeper, faster, or against an opponent?

Early rungs answer questions 1 to 4 (make it work). Late rungs answer 6 to 9 (make it good). Stop as soon as the answer matches the real product.

## 4. How it gets fixed: the "So" catalog

Seventeen design moves, plus one teaching device (the analogy map), cover 82 of the 86 fixes. The last column links each move to the matching principle in TRIZ (Altshuller's theory of inventive problem solving, see section 11), so you can study each move further.

| Move | Count | What it means | Examples from the videos | How to show it | TRIZ principle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Swap the medium** | 13 | Change the material, working fluid or energy source | water → refrigerant; rope → steam → fuel; wood → stone → steel; flame → electricity; rubber → ceramic | same object, new colour or texture | #35 parameter change, #36 phase change |
| **Reshape** | 13 | Change the geometry | star-shaped core; blunt nose; wedge-cut stones; U-profile with zipper edges; ramp washers; ball → flat discs; coiled pipe | morph the part in the same camera view | #14 curvature, #17 another dimension, #4 asymmetry |
| **Constrain** | 8 | Guide, hold or anchor the thing that misbehaves | guide posts; hold the nut; anchor the mine; guide rails; valve seat | add one part in a contrasting colour | (no single principle) |
| **Push a parameter** | 6 | Raise one number past a threshold | 60 Hz → tens of kHz (silent); thousands → millions → billions of flips per second | a dial, a counter, an oscilloscope whose wave gets denser | #35 parameter change, #18 vibration |
| **Combine / layer** | 6 | Stack or fuse several solutions | three sensors in one mine; double wall with clay; many turbine stages; repeated chip layers | exploded view, stack building up | #5 merging, #7 nested doll, #40 composites |
| **Automate** | 5 | A machine takes over the human's job | motor spins the magnet; steam valve lifts the hammer; pendulum works the latches | the hand leaves the frame | trend: less human involvement |
| **Sense and react** | 4 | Add a sensor and a response | contact horns, magnetic, acoustic and pressure sensors; ABS; current flipped at the right point | a sensor "eye" and a flash when it fires | #23 feedback |
| **Field instead of motion** | 4 | Replace a moving part with a switching electric or magnetic field | induction coil; maglev electromagnets; microwave field; chip gate | poles swapping colour: "a magnet that moves without going anywhere" | #28 mechanics substitution |
| **Close the loop** | 4 | Catch what escapes and reuse it | refrigerant cycle; ball return tube; fixed blades redirect steam; smoke routed under the floor | loop arrow, same particles going round | #34 discard and recover, #20 continuity |
| **Make the problem do the work** | 3 | Use the problem's own energy or the system's own action | falling hammer ignites the fuel; loosening motion tightens the wedge washers; hot smoke rising pulls the flow | show the "enemy" force, then the same force doing the useful work | #25 self-service, #22 blessing in disguise |
| **Trade one quantity for another** | 3 | Give up distance for force, or voltage for current | rope on a drum; hydraulic brake pistons; welding transformer | long thin arrow becomes short fat arrow | #29 hydraulics |
| **Split the functions** | 2 | Two opposite needs, separated in time or space | streamlined cap in flight, blunt nose at impact; fire moved outside the room | two-state animation (before / at impact) | separation principles; #2 taking out, #3 local quality |
| **Temporary support** | 2 | A scaffold that is removed afterwards | wooden centering under the arch; a dam holding back water | scaffold appears, then is pulled away | #27 cheap short-lived objects |
| **Scale up** | 2 | More of the same element | posts along a bridge; a track lined with magnets | repeat the element in a row | |
| **Periodic release** | 1 | Store energy, release it in steps | pendulum escapement | a tick-tock rhythm | #19 periodic action |
| **Balance** | 1 | Counter a load with an opposite load | elevator counterweight | seesaw | #8 anti-weight |
| **Fail-safe** | 1 | Make the failure itself trigger the protection | rope snaps → teeth bite the rail | cut the rope on purpose and show it holding | #11 cushion in advance |
| **Analogy map** | 4 | Explain the next process step with an everyday experience | sticker tan → light exposure; rusty coin in cola → etching; mist on a cold can → layer growth | split screen or hard cut, analogy → machine, same framing | (a teaching device, not an invention move) |

The remaining 4 fixes were one-offs (reduce, modularize, fuse, apply).

**Key observation:** *swap the medium* and *reshape* together make up 30% of all fixes. They are also the easiest to see: the viewer notices immediately that the wood became stone or the ball became a flat disc. When you have a choice, use a visible move.

## 5. The deep patterns: eight ideas that recur

1. **Swap or reshape before you add.** Most improvements change *what it is made of* or *what shape it has*, not how many parts it has. Adding parts comes late (sensors, rails, counterweights).

2. **Replace motion with a switching field.** Shaking a magnet → flipping the current in a coil (induction). Moving magnets → electromagnets flipped at the right moment (maglev). Shaking a box → flipping an electric field (microwave). The viewer already accepts that motion makes heat or force; the trick is to show a field that "moves without going anywhere."

3. **Turn the dial past a threshold.** One number is pushed up step by step until something qualitatively new happens: hum disappears above hearing range; molecules can no longer follow the field and start colliding. Show the dial and the threshold. A repeated "still not enough… still not enough… now!" builds suspense cheaply.

4. **Catch what escapes.** Whatever flies away (refrigerant, balls, leftover steam, hot smoke) is captured and reused. This turns a one-shot trick into a machine that runs forever.

5. **Make the problem do the work.** The most satisfying endings flip the enemy into the engine: the falling hammer ignites its own fuel; the force that would loosen the nut tightens it; rising smoke pulls the flow through the floor; a weight on the other side carries half the load. When you can end on this, do.

6. **Split the conflict.** When you need two opposite things, separate them in **time** (streamlined in flight, blunt at impact; strong thrust at liftoff, weaker later) or **space** (thick stones near the fire, thin ones far away; fire outside the room, smoke inside the floor).

7. **Trade what you have too much of for what you lack.** Door hinge, syringes, rope drum, hydraulic brakes and the welding transformer are all the same deal: give up distance (or voltage) to gain force (or current). One analogy can unlock several inventions.

8. **Arms races end in layers.** When something fights back (an enemy navy, vibration, water pressure, floods), each fix invites a counter-move, and the final design combines several defences (the mine that checks magnetism, sound *and* pressure).

And one direction runs through almost all of them: **the human is removed step by step.** Shaking by hand → motor → no moving parts. Waiting by the wire → contact horns → sensors. Hauling the rock → steam valve → a hammer that runs itself.

## 6. What "fully optimized" looks like

The final rung almost never says "and now it is better." It says **what is gone**:

- "No shaking required." (induction)
- "No wheels, no friction." (maglev)
- "Hard against hard, nothing wears down." (faucet)
- A hammer that drives piles "on its own, without pause." (diesel hammer)
- The loosening motion becomes "a force that locks it even tighter." (bolt)

This matches what TRIZ calls the **Ideal Final Result**: the job gets done with no added effort, cost, wear or person. So aim your last rung at that sentence: *"Now ___ happens by itself, with no ___."*

**The scar map.** In a well-built chain, every part of the final product is a scar from one failure:

| Elevator part | Exists because |
|---|---|
| Safety teeth on the rail | the rope can snap |
| Electric motor | hand and steam hoists were too slow and needed a boiler |
| Counterweight | the motor burned out lifting the full car |
| Guide rails | the car swayed |
| Floor buttons | it had to stop at exactly the right floor |

Draw this table for any invention **before** you write the script. It tells you which rungs you need. A part with no failure behind it either needs a rung or should not be mentioned.

## 7. The method, step by step

**Step 1: Name the job.** Verb + object, without the product name. "Heat water", not "microwave oven". Test: could a child act out the job on a desk?

**Step 2: State the core principle in one sentence.** What physical effect does the final design rely on? ("A changing magnetic field shakes the metal and heats it." "The same pressure on a bigger piston gives a bigger force.") If you cannot say it in one sentence, you do not yet understand the invention well enough to simplify it.

**Step 3: Find rung 1, the dumbest thing that works a little.** Choose one of four starting points:
- *Caveman version:* what would someone do with bare hands, a rock, a log, a rope? (drop a rock on the pile, float a barrel, lay a log)
- *Toy experiment:* the core principle done by hand in the crudest way (shake a magnet, spray and fan a box, blow on a pinwheel)
- *Everyday analogy:* something the viewer has already felt (a door near the hinge, a sunburn around a sticker)
- *Wrong intuition:* what most people would guess, which turns out wrong (sharp tip to pierce armor)

It must (a) show the core principle, (b) fit on a desk, (c) fail visibly within seconds.

**Step 4: Draw the scar map of the real product.** List every important part of the final design. For each, ask: *"What failure would make someone add this?"* Each answer is a candidate rung.

**Step 5: Order the rungs** using the question ladder in section 3 (works → stays put → no person → strong enough → no waste → no side effect → lasts → controllable → scales). Each rung's fix must leave the next flaw visible.

**Step 6: Pick one move per rung** from the catalog in section 4. One change per rung, never two. Prefer visible moves (material, shape, an added part). When the move is invisible (a field, a frequency), give it a visible stand-in: colour, a dial, an oscilloscope, arrows.

**Step 7: Prune to 3 to 7 rungs.** Merge rungs that teach the same thing. Keep a dead end only if its failure explains the principle by contrast (the vibrating plate that did *not* heat the water shows that random collisions are what matter). Put the biggest conceptual jump (the "Then a genius engineer…" moment) two-thirds of the way in.

**Step 8: Write the But/So script** (template in section 9.3). Each rung: one sentence for the design, one for why it works, one for the "But", one for the "So".

**Step 9: Run the logic check.**
- Does every "So" fix exactly the "But" before it?
- Does the viewer have everything needed to follow each step from what came earlier?
- Does every part of the final design appear in the scar map?
- Is every "why it works" sentence physically true? (section 10)

## 8. Two worked examples (inventions not in the set)

### 8.1 The toilet U-bend

**Job:** carry waste away without letting sewer smell back into the house.
**Core principle:** a pool of water in a bent pipe blocks gas but lets waste through.
**Shape:** A (history ladder) or B (toy experiment with a clear pipe and coloured smoke).

| # | Design | But | So (move) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A hole over a pit | The smell rises straight into the house (side effect) | Carry the waste far away through a pipe (split functions in space) |
| 2 | A straight pipe to the sewer | Sewer gas flows back up the open pipe (side effect) | Put a flap on the pipe (constrain) |
| 3 | A flap valve | Waste jams it, it rots and leaks (wear) | Bend the pipe into a U so water stays in the bottom and blocks the gas (reshape) |
| 4 | U-bend water seal | A big rush of water down the drain sucks the seal out, and the smell is back (control) | Add a vent pipe to the roof that lets air in behind the water (balance the pressure) |
| Final | U-bend + vent pipe | | |

**Scar map:** pipe ← smell in the house; U-bend ← flaps jam and leak; vent ← the seal gets sucked out.
**Visual stand-ins:** green smoke for sewer gas; blue water in a clear pipe; an arrow pulling the water out for the siphon; bubbles entering through the vent.
**Last line:** "A door made of water. It never wears out, and every flush replaces it." (makes the problem do the work)

### 8.2 Noise-cancelling headphones

**Job:** hear your music, not the plane.
**Core principle:** a sound wave plus its mirror image adds up to silence.
**Shape:** B (toy experiment: two ropes shaken in opposite directions).

| # | Design | But | So (move) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Fingers in your ears | Blocks the noise, and the music too (doesn't work) | Sealed cups with a speaker inside: block outside sound, play the inside (split functions) |
| 2 | Sealed ear cups | High hiss is blocked, but deep engine rumble passes through the walls (too weak) | Thicker, heavier cups (scale up) |
| 3 | Heavy cups | Heavy and hot, and the deepest rumble still gets in (side effect) | Fight sound with sound: an outside microphone hears the rumble, the speaker plays the mirror-image wave (sense and react) |
| 4 | Outside microphone + anti-noise | What reaches the ear inside the cup is not exactly what the outside mic heard (fit, hair, glasses), so some noise is left (control) | A second microphone inside the cup listens to what is left and keeps correcting (sense and react, feedback) |
| Final | Light sealed cups block the high sounds, two-microphone anti-noise cancels the low ones | | combine / layer |

**Scar map:** sealed cups ← fingers blocked the music; anti-noise chip ← thick cups were heavy and still leaked rumble; inside microphone ← the outside one guessed wrong.
**Visual stand-ins:** sound as a rope wave across the desk; anti-noise as a mirrored rope in a second colour; the two together as a flat rope. Noise red, anti-noise blue, music green.
**Last line:** "Silence, made out of sound."

## 9. How to present it visually

### 9.1 The video grammar used in these shorts

1. **One desk, toy scale.** Almost every machine is rebuilt as a desk model: bridges, pile drivers, elevators, rockets (the armor-piercing shell, shown full-scale in a desert, is the one clear exception). Real scale (a real photo, a full-size site, a clock tower, the actual appliance) appears only at the end, as a pull-back.
2. **Same camera, one change per rung.** The underwater-pillars video keeps the same view of the same ring around the same pillar, and only the material or shape changes. The ondol video rebuilds one room model step by step. The eye finds the difference instantly.
3. **Failure is an event, not a sentence.** Leaking droplets, logs swept away, stones tumbling, a skid streak on the desk, a shell bursting, a gear stalling. If you cannot animate the failure, the rung is too abstract.
4. **Make the invisible visible, with one fixed colour code.** Red = heat; red/blue = hot/cold (or magnetic poles, but only one meaning per video); arrows sized by force; smoke or steam to trace flow; coloured liquid inside syringes; atoms and molecules as balls; an orderly grid (no heat) versus a chaotic jumble (heat).
5. **Measure the pushed number on screen.** When the move is "push a parameter", an oscilloscope wave gets denser at each step.
6. **Cutaways and see-through casings** whenever the action is inside (faucet, brakes, mine, transformer, washers).
7. **The thinking beat marks the big jump, not every step.** The cartoon engineer's close-up (hand on chin, eyes behind blue glasses) appears at the one or two moments where the principle changes: "Then a genius engineer…", "Instead of a ball…", "Engineers wrestled with the problem…". Ordinary fixes get no thinking beat.
8. **The new part gets a contrasting colour** (bright orange guide rails, blue nylon in the nut).
9. **Analogy split.** Act out the everyday version with real props (a coin in cola, a cold can), then hard-cut to the technical part in the same framing.
10. **Captions:** burned in, centred, 1 to 6 words.
11. **Payoff demo:** prove the final design works (the big syringe crushes a can; ice cubes appear; molecules collide).
12. **Reveal the name last, or never.** End on the benefit; let the title carry the name.

### 9.2 Static formats (slides, posters, documents)

| Format | What it is | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Ladder strip** | A comic strip: one panel per rung, left to right, same viewpoint. A red ✕ marks where each version fails, the "But" is the caption under it, and an arrow labelled with the "So" leads to the next panel. | Any chain; the default |
| **But/So ledger** | The table from each micro file: Design / Why it works / But / So. | Handouts, study notes |
| **Scar map** | A drawing of the final design with callouts. Each callout names the failure that forced that part and is coloured to match its rung in the ladder strip. | The final slide; shows the whole story in one image |
| **One-number line** | Plot a single measure across the rungs (temperature reached, frequency, force, thrust) with a horizontal threshold line ("ice", "silent", "molecules collide"). | "Push a parameter" chains (induction, microwave, ice) |
| **Onion skin** | Each new version drawn over a faded ghost of the previous one, changed part highlighted. | Shape and material changes (faucet, bridge, washers) |
| **Contradiction card** | "We want ___, but that makes ___ worse." Below it: how it was separated (in time / in space / by condition). | Splitting moves (armor-piercing cap, star-shaped core, thick/thin stones) |
| **Move icons** | A small fixed icon for each move (swap, reshape, guide, dial, loop, flip, sensor, stack, scaffold…) placed on each "So" arrow. Across a series, viewers learn to recognize the moves themselves. | A series of explainers |

### 9.3 Script template

```
HOOK (≤ 5 s)
  "What happens if you ___?"            (toy experiment)
  "Long ago, people ___."                (history)
  "You'd think ___, right? In fact ___." (puzzle)
  "If you ___, you'll notice ___."       (analogy)

RUNG (≈ 10-15 s each, 3-7 times)
  "___ works, because ___."
  "But ___."                              ← show it happen
  "So ___."                               ← one change only

BIG JUMP (once, about two-thirds in)
  "Then someone thought: instead of ___, what if ___?"

FINAL (≈ 15-20 s)
  "Now ___. No more ___."                 ← what is gone
  "That's ___."                           ← name, or leave it to the title
```

## 10. Honesty: simplify, don't falsify

The videos are **logical reconstructions**, not history. That is what makes them easy to follow, and it is fine, as long as the result stays true. The "Accuracy notes" at the bottom of each micro file show three kinds of liberty:

- **History compressed or reordered** (acceptable if not presented as history). Latch escapements came centuries *before* the pendulum; arc welding came before flame welding; the microwave oven came from noticing that a radar tube melted a candy bar, not from stepping up a frequency.
- **Loose mechanism words** (avoid). Induction heats through electric currents swirling in the pot base, not "friction of atoms". A transformer trades voltage for current; it does not keep the current unchanged. Explosives detonate, while rocket propellant burns.
- **Missing conditions** (add a line). The wedge washers only work because their ramp is steeper than the thread; an arch also needs solid ground at its feet to push against; ABS mainly keeps the car steerable.

**Rules:**
1. Present the ladder as logic ("engineers had to solve…"), not as dated history, unless you have checked the history.
2. Use real names and dates only when verified (the elevator video uses Otis 1852 and Siemens 1880).
3. A simplification may leave things out. It must not state a wrong cause. Have someone who knows the field check each "why it works" sentence.
4. Put the real technical names in the description or a pinned comment, for viewers who want to go further.

## 11. Going deeper: TRIZ

The move catalog in section 4 is a small, visual subset of **TRIZ**, the theory of inventive problem solving developed by Genrich Altshuller from studying patents. Four TRIZ ideas match what the videos do:

| TRIZ idea | Where it appears in the videos |
|---|---|
| **Contradiction**: improving one thing makes another worse | every "But" (more explosive → shell breaks; blunt nose → drag) |
| **Separation principles**: resolve a contradiction in time, space or condition | breakaway cap (time); fire outside, smoke under the floor (space) |
| **Ideal Final Result**: the function happens by itself, at no cost | the endings in section 6 |
| **Trends of evolution**: systems move toward fewer humans, from mechanics to fields, toward more control | hand → motor → field (induction, microwave, maglev); waiting by a wire → automatic sensors (mine); fixed → controlled (ABS, mixing faucet) |

A readable starting point is Altshuller's own popular book *And Suddenly the Inventor Appeared*. TRIZ's list of 40 inventive principles is a much larger move catalog. Use it when none of the 17 moves in section 4 fits.
