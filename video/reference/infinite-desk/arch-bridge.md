# Arch bridge: each material or shape fixes the last weakness until small wedge-cut stones hold each other up

**Chain at a glance:** Log across a stream -> Timber bridge on posts -> Huge stone slabs -> Small stones stepped inward -> Angled stones leaning together -> Arch built on wooden support

**The simple problem:** Get across a stream without wading, and keep the crossing standing as the water gets wider. [00:00]

## The ladder

### 1. A log [00:00]
- **Idea:** Lay a log across the stream.
- **Why it works:** One trunk spans a narrow gap.
- **But:** Waterways get wider [00:02], and one log no longer reaches (implied). - `doesn't-scale`
- **So:** Build a proper bridge out of many pieces of wood [00:02]. - `scale-up`
- **Shown as:** A low-poly model landscape with a stream between grassy banks; no log is visible in the sampled frames.

### 2. Timber bridge [00:02]
- **Idea:** Posts in the riverbed carry a deck of logs.
- **Why it works:** Many supports let wood cross a wider waterway.
- **But:** Wood rots and floods sweep it away [00:06]. - `wear-or-leak`
- **So:** Build from strong stone instead [00:09]. - `substitute-medium`
- **Shown as:** The log deck on posts breaks apart and logs drop into the water with a splash.
![Timber bridge swept away](frames/arch-bridge/f003_0-00-09.jpg)

### 3. Huge stone slabs [00:09]
- **Idea:** Lay large flat stones on stone piers.
- **Why it works:** Stone does not rot and stands up to floods.
- **But:** Finding and transporting stones that big is extremely difficult [00:12]. - `manual-effort`
- **So:** Stack many smaller stones instead [00:16]. - `other:modularize`
- **Shown as:** A big slab resting on a stone pier, then a chain of massive boulders across the water.
![Stone slab bridge](frames/arch-bridge/f004_0-00-12.jpg)

### 4. Small stones stepped inward [00:16]
- **Idea:** Stack layers from both banks, each one reaching slightly further toward the middle [00:19].
- **Why it works:** Each stone rests partly on the one below, so small stones can close a gap.
- **But:** The wider the gap, the further the top stones must stick out, until the bridge collapses [00:24]. - `doesn't-scale`
- **So:** Cut the sides of each stone at an angle so neighbours lean against one another [00:30]. - `reshape`
- **Shown as:** Stone courses step out over the water from both banks, then the stacked span stands over a dark gap just before the collapse is narrated.
![Stones stepped inward from both banks](frames/arch-bridge/f006_0-00-20.jpg)

### 5. Angled stones leaning together [00:30]
- **Idea:** Fit wedge-shaped stones into a curve so each presses on its neighbours [00:32].
- **Why it works:** Weight becomes squeezing along the curve, and stone is very strong when squeezed.
- **But:** Until the final stone goes in, the others simply fall [00:35]. - `incomplete`
- **So:** Build a temporary arch-shaped wooden support first [00:39]. - `temporary-support`
- **Shown as:** A block has its sides cut; a curved strip of stones carries white arrows pointing along the curve (stones pushing on each other); then a half-built arch sheds blocks into the water.
![Arrows show the stones pressing together](frames/arch-bridge/f009_0-00-35.jpg)

### Final: Stone arch bridge [00:43]
- **Idea:** Stones are laid one by one on the wooden frame, the last one into the centre [00:45]; when the frame is removed they press on each other and the bridge stands [00:47]. Smaller arches through the sides let flood water pass [00:54].
- **Why it solves the chain:** Small, easily moved stones (rung 3) that cannot topple off an overhang (rung 4) because they lean together (rung 5), assembled safely on a removable support; the side openings answer the floods that destroyed the timber bridge (rung 2).
- **Reveal:** The closing line names it, "that is how the arch bridge was created" [00:58], as the toy model with small side arches cuts to a real photograph of a long stone arch bridge with open side arches reflected in a river.
![Final model with flood arches](frames/arch-bridge/f015_0-00-58.jpg)

## Accuracy notes
- Real names: rung 4 is a corbelled ("false") arch; the wedge stones are voussoirs, the centre stone the keystone, the wooden support the centering; the side openings are open spandrels, as on the Zhaozhou Bridge in China (about 600 AD), which the final photo resembles.
- The video omits that an arch pushes outward at its feet and needs solid abutments; the keystone is not structurally special, it just closes the ring.
- The order is a tidy story, not strict history: corbelled and true arches coexisted for millennia, and brick arches existed long before stone arch bridges.
