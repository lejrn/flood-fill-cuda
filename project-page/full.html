Abstract

flood-fill-cuda finds every red blob in an image and recolors it on the GPU, in Python with Numba CUDA. It is a breadth-first search (BFS) all the way. It starts from a plain CPU BFS, chapter 0, and each chapter answers a weakness an earlier one measured.

Chapters 1 to 3 spread one BFS over one block, then two blocks, then the whole GPU. Chapter 4 fills two blobs in one launch. Chapter 5 finds every blob by itself: 755,577 blobs in 24.8 ms on a 16-megapixel noise image, with no seeds given.

All of them move one pixel per step, and every step ends at a grid-wide barrier. Chapters 7 and 8 keep the BFS and change the step to a tile of 32 × 32 pixels. A 64-megapixel blob then takes 127 rounds, not 4,001 levels: 3.107 ms where the pixel BFS took 70.56 ms.

Chapter 8 labels many blobs the same way. The 81-megapixel test image, input_blobs.png, has 2,522 blobs. It is recolored in 2.40 ms from RGB, where chapter 5's pixel BFS took 47.06 ms in the same session.

Chapter 9 takes the tiles to all 16 CPU threads and labels the image in 21.06 ms. By kernel time the GPU is still 9.43× faster; counting the copies to and from the GPU, the CPU wins by 2.34×.

One session sets the project beside OpenCV and CuPy. Chapter 8 writes a label map 6× faster than CuPy and 24× to 30× faster than OpenCV on 16 threads, in GPU time.

Chapter 6 is the one chapter that is not a BFS. It labels runs of pixels with a union-find, and it stays on this page as a reference. On the test image it ties with chapter 8 once both read the image with the same kernel.

Every kernel is checked pixel for pixel against a CPU reference, and the reference is checked against OpenCV. Every timing comes from a committed benchmark file, and the page shows where the GPU loses too.

Ten Chapters, One Chain

Each chapter starts from a weakness an earlier one measured. The second column names it. GPU times are kernel times, with the input already on the GPU. Chapter 6 steps off the BFS line; it is kept as a reference.

Chapter What the last chapter left open What it built Headline
0. CPU BFS Nothing yet. This is the starting point. A pure-Python BFS and a compiled (@njit) BFS, one pixel at a time on one core. The reference every kernel must match, pixel for pixel.
1. One block One CPU core is serial. Level-synchronous BFS in one block, with a shared-memory ring and a global spill tier. 1.8-2.0× compiled CPU code (@njit) on a 36 Mpx blob, across two runs.
2. Two blocks One block uses one SM (streaming multiprocessor), 4% of the GPU. Two blocks and three ways to share one wave: by space, by one global queue, by direction. 2.10× chapter 1 at 36 Mpx. The plain global queue won.
3. N blocks Two blocks use two SMs, 8%. One cooperative grid across all 24 SMs, grid-wide barriers, then 8-connectivity. 64 Mpx in 100.3 ms. Registers cap the timed kernel at 512 threads per SM; a leaner twin without counters fits 768.
4. Two blobs Each launch fills one blob. Two blobs in one launch. The label rides in a spare bit of each queue entry. 1.05-2.05× faster than two back-to-back launches (median, four scenes).
5. N blobs The caller must supply the seeds. Seedless discovery: candidate waves plus an atomicMin union-find. 755,577 blobs in 24.8 ms on 16 Mpx of noise, one launch, no seeds.
6. N runs, not a BFS Every kernel still moves pixels. Is a search needed at all? Run-based labeling: pack the image to one bit per pixel, then six plain kernels. 58.51 ms to 2.96 ms from RGB, or 1.46 ms from a packed mask, on the 81 Mpx image. Same session.
7. One blob, tiles A BFS still advances one pixel per step. One blob, a BFS over 32 × 32 tiles. Each tile is closed in registers. A 64 Mpx square in 3.107 ms, 22.7× chapter 3's best grid. Chapter 6 is still faster there, at 2.252 ms.
8. N blobs, tiles The tiles fill one seeded blob. Tile components as BFS nodes: waves of labels, one union pass, a path for dense tiles. The 81 Mpx image in 2.403 ms, 0.890 of chapter 6's time. With the same pack kernel on both sides it is a tie, 0.982.
9. Every CPU core Every CPU number so far used one core. The ideas of chapters 3, 7 and 8 on all 16 CPU threads, as compiled parallel loops with no atomics. The 64 Mpx square in 21.29 ms, 51× one core. The GPU is 6.24× faster by kernel time; with the copies the CPU wins by 1.89×.

The CPU walks, the GPU sweeps

One block is only 1.9× faster than the CPU, and two blocks 3.9×. With all 24 SMs the GPU finishes in 134 ms, 15.3× faster than the CPU's 2,041 ms.

Tiles, Not Pixels

Chapters 1 to 5 spread one pixel per level, and every level ends at grid-wide barriers. Chapter 3 pays two of them per level: 8,002 on a 64-megapixel square.

Chapter 7 keeps the BFS and changes its step. It cuts the image into tiles of 32 × 32 pixels, and one warp of 32 threads takes a tile. Each thread holds one row of the tile as 32 bits in a register.

The warp fills along the rows, the diagonals and the columns until nothing changes. Only the tile's border bits go on, into the tiles around it, and those tiles join the next round. Today's kernel needs one barrier per round.

Many blobs: pieces and waves

Chapter 8 labels many blobs the same way. First every tile finds the blob pieces inside it, in registers, all tiles at once.

Some pieces are seeds. Each seed sends a wave from piece to piece, one round at a time. Where two waves meet, the meeting is noted, and at the end each blob keeps its smallest label.

On the whole 81-megapixel image that is 36,863 pieces, 11,609 seeds and 6 rounds.

Every Stage on the Same Image

Every stage ran on the same image, input_blobs.png: 81 million pixels and 2,522 red blobs.

Pure Python, given the seeds, needs 24,083 ms. Compiled CPU code (Numba @njit) needs 1,346 ms. Chapter 5 needs 51.03 ms and chapter 8 needs 2.40 ms.

The chart shows orders of magnitude, not one ratio. Its two ends are different contracts: the pure-Python bar is a single run from RGB on the host clock, and the GPU bars are kernel time.

The like-for-like pair comes from chapter 9, in one session. One CPU core labels the image in 271.5 ms and the GPU in 2.23 ms by kernel time, 122×. With the copies to and from the GPU it takes 49.3 ms, 5.5×.

  • With the copies: a host image to a host label map through chapter 8 takes 59.98 ms. That is 1.8× faster than OpenCV on 16 threads and 1.6× slower than chapter 9.

The Reference That Is Not a BFS

Chapter 6 asks a different question: why move pixels at all? It drops the wavefront. A run is one unbroken span of red inside a row, and the test image has 13.45 million red pixels but only 539,207 runs.

Chapter 6 builds a table of runs, joins the runs that touch across rows with a union-find, and paints. This is connected-component labeling (CCL), not a search: it has no levels, no frontier and no barrier.

It stays here because it is a strong yardstick for the tiles.

In its own chapter and session it reads 2.96 ms from RGB and 1.46 ms from a mask already packed to one bit per pixel. The full account is in chapter 6's own story.

How the Numbers Were Measured

  • One machine: an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 Laptop GPU with 24 SMs and an Intel Core i9-13900H, under WSL2 with 16 CPU threads.
  • Correctness comes first. Every kernel is checked against a CPU reference built with Numba @njit. The tests compare depth maps or label maps, not pixel counts.
  • The reference has an outside anchor. It is checked against OpenCV's connectedComponents, and against a BFS written as whole-array dilations.
  • GPU times are kernel times, with the input already on the GPU. Copies and allocation are not included, unless a line says "with the copies" or "end to end".
  • Chapters 1 to 5 time the launch and synchronize on the host. Chapters 6 to 9 and the library session time their GPU sides with CUDA events, after a clock warm-up.
  • The two benchmark images are in the repository, with their checksums.
  • Results drift between sessions on this laptop: chapter 5 on input_blobs.png read 51.03 ms in one session and 58.51 ms in another. So every comparison pairs numbers from one session where it can.
  • Every result is a committed JSON file under src/flood_fill_cuda/results/.

Related Links

Breadth-first search on the GPU goes back to Harish and Narayanan (2007). Merrill, Garland and Grimshaw (2012) show how much redundant work a 2D lattice causes without duplicate culling. That is why chapters 1 to 5 claim a pixel before they enqueue it.

The enqueue in chapters 2 to 5, and in chapter 1's spill variant, uses warp-aggregated atomics: one atomic per warp instead of one per thread. The persistent kernels of chapters 2 to 5 wait on Numba's cooperative groups grid barrier.

Connected-component labeling on the GPU has a long line of work: Hawick, Leist and Playne (2010), Stava and Benes (2011), and Playne and Hawick (2018). The block-based BUF and BKE algorithms of Allegretti, Bolelli and Grana (2020) belong to it too. Chapter 5's union-find follows this line.

The library baselines are OpenCV's connectedComponents and CuPy's cupyx.scipy.ndimage.label.

Labeling runs instead of pixels is a classic CPU idea, described by He, Chao and Suzuki (2008). Chapter 6 applies it on the GPU.

Chapter 6 skips the decoupled look-back scan of Merrill and Garland (2016). There are too few rows for a multi-block scan to pay.

Every kernel is written in Python with Numba for CUDA GPUs.
