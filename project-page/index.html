flood-fill-cuda: A GPU Flood Fill, from Pixels to Tiles

Find every red blob in an image and recolor it, as a breadth-first search on the GPU. Python and Numba CUDA, on one laptop GPU.

Transcript

Footage of a drone light show, then its filter output beside it: white blobs on black. A stopwatch shows the frame budget, 33 ms. A camera films a drone show; a filter turns each drone into a white blob. Count them all, every frame: thirty-three milliseconds.

A card for the drone frames: the CPU's bar at 0.679 ms, far inside the budget, and a question mark for the GPU. Then a card for an 81-megapixel frame: the CPU's stopwatch runs past 33 ms to 225 ms. On frames this small, compiled CPU code keeps up. Scale up: eighty-one megapixels, same thirty-three milliseconds. The CPU needs over a fifth of a second. Where does the GPU win? Two places: size and count.

The picture freezes and zooms into one drone until its pixels show as squares. Freeze. Zoom into one drone until its pixels show.

Pixels turn from red to painted one at a time, on a CPU board with one core lit. Beside it a GPU board with 24 SM tiles, all dark. On the CPU: paint a pixel, queue its red neighbours, repeat. One pixel per step, on one core, while twenty-four GPU SMs sit idle.

One SM tile lights up and a whole ring of pixels is painted in one step. A race bar: on one small blob the CPU wins, 4.3× (0.121 against 0.520 ms). So move it onto one SM, its queue in the SM's shared memory: two hundred fifty-six workers, called threads, in one block, paint a whole ring at once. But on one small blob, the CPU still wins. So: size first.

A giant blob. The block's queue outgrows its 48 KB of shared memory and the rest spills into global memory. On one giant blob, that queue overflows: a block gets only forty-eight kilobytes of shared memory, and this queue needs over a hundred. So the overflow spills to global memory: the card's eight gigabytes.

One block is 1.1× faster than the CPU. One of the 24 SM tiles is lit. Now one block is a little faster than the CPU. But that's one SM of twenty-four.

Two blocks, blue and green, on two SMs. The queue moves into global memory. Twice as fast as one block, and 22 SM tiles stay dark. So add a second block on a second SM. Squeezed onto one SM, two blocks ran one point seven times slower. But shared memory is private to its block. So the whole queue moves to global memory: every new pixel's address is written there. On big blobs, measured, that's no slower. Two blocks are twice as fast. But twenty-two SMs stay dark.

All 24 SMs light up with 48 blocks, one colour per block, and the colours mix into speckle on the blob. 8.5× the CPU. A sweep of other launch shapes gains 15% at best. So use every SM: forty-eight blocks, two on each. Eight and a half times the CPU. Each colour is one block: they share one queue, so their pixels mix, and small rings keep only a few busy. We also tried other threads per block and block counts: on a smaller square, the best gained fifteen percent. We kept forty-eight by two hundred fifty-six.

A thin winding snake. One block beats 48: a gate closes twice per ring, about 2 microseconds each time. But on a thin snake, forty-eight blocks lose to one. Why? Every ring, the chip stops twice at a gate, two microseconds each.

The wave becomes a square and needs half the rings. The scoreboard's second row: one huge blob, GPU 11×. So pass fewer gates: step diagonally too. On a square, half the rings, half the gates.

The 81-megapixel frame with its 2,522 blobs. One launch per blob, about 1,462 ms, an estimate. The CPU, at 202 ms, wins by 7.2×. Size is won. Now count: twenty-five hundred blobs in the big frame, one launch each. Slower than the CPU: we built for size, not count.

A queue entry with one spare bit as a tag, and two blobs filling in two colours in one launch. About 850 ms: the CPU still wins, by 4.2×. So tag each queue entry with its blob: one spare bit of the entry's word, in global memory. Each new pixel inherits the tag, and the tag picks its colour: its label. One bit tells two blobs apart: two per launch, faster. But still slower than the CPU: only two seeds, each handed in.

Waves start all over the frame at once. 53.9 ms: 3.8× faster than the CPU, but over the 33 ms budget. So the GPU finds its own seeds: every blob in one fill launch, about fifty milliseconds. Faster than the CPU. But over budget: every red pixel still rides the queue.

The picture is cut into tiles of 32 by 32 pixels. A close-up of one tile: 32 threads, one row each, fill along the rows, the diagonals and the columns until nothing changes. So the queue carries tiles, not pixels: squares of thirty-two by thirty-two. One warp, thirty-two threads that run in step, takes a tile. Each thread holds one row, one bit per pixel. The row sits in registers: the thread's own memory, closer than shared memory. Fill along every row at once, step diagonally, fill along the columns, and repeat until nothing changes.

Border bits pass into the neighbouring tiles and the wave crosses the giant square tile by tile. 4,001 rings become 127 rounds, and 101 ms becomes 3.12 ms. Row two: GPU 340×. Only the tile's border bits go out, into the tiles around it, and those join the next round's queue. One gate per round, not two per ring. On the giant square, four thousand rings become a hundred twenty-seven rounds: from a hundred milliseconds to three. But that's one blob, from one seed.

A 384-pixel crop of the frame: 135 pieces inside its tiles, 36 of them seeds, 8 blobs. Coloured waves spread from piece to piece and meet. So first, every tile finds the blob pieces inside it, in registers, all at once. Some pieces are seeds: each seed sends a wave from piece to piece, one round at a time. Where two waves meet, the meeting is noted, and at the end each blob keeps its smallest label.

The view pulls back to the whole frame: 11,609 seeds, 6 rounds, 2,522 blobs, 2.21 ms. Row three: GPU 100×. On the big frame: over eleven thousand seeds, six rounds, every blob counted. Two point two milliseconds, once the frame is on the card: inside the budget.

The scoreboard. One small blob: 0.192 against 0.044 ms, GPU 4.4× in kernel time; with the copy to the card the CPU wins, 3.5×. One huge blob: 1,065 against 3.12 ms, 340×. Many blobs: 225 against 2.21 ms, 100×. The drones return, then the title card: flood fill, from pixels to tiles. One huge blob, or many at once: the GPU. And one small blob? In kernel time, the GPU now; with the copy to the card, still the CPU. Every drone, every frame.

One Pixel per Step

A CPU paints one pixel, queues its red neighbors, and repeats. One GPU block of 256 threads paints a whole ring per step.

The same blob, recorded from both. A step is one loop iteration, not a time.

The Whole GPU

One block uses one of the GPU's 24 SMs. Chapter 3 runs 48 blocks on all of them, sharing one queue. Stepping diagonally too halves the levels.

Chapter 7

Tiles, Not Pixels

Every step so far moved one pixel and ended at a grid-wide barrier. Chapter 7 keeps the BFS and makes the step a tile of 32 × 32 pixels. One warp of 32 threads closes a tile in registers: each thread holds one row as 32 bits.

One real tile of the test image, sweep by sweep, replayed on the GPU by the kernel's own functions.

Filling along a row takes five steps. f is the row's reached bits and m its red bits, so a bit travels through red pixels only.

A window of 41 × 41 tiles on a solid square. Right: as recorded. Left: a pixel BFS after the same number of steps, drawn from its level rule.

Against the Libraries

The same work through OpenCV on the CPU and CuPy on the GPU, in one session.

  • In GPU time, chapter 8 is 6.2× faster than CuPy and 30× faster than OpenCV on 16 threads.
  • With the copies, it is 1.8× faster than OpenCV and 1.6× slower than chapter 9 on the CPU.
  • On pixel noise OpenCV wins: 24.83 ms on 16 threads against chapter 9's 27.63 ms.

The Fine Print

Chapter 6: the reference that is not a BFS

Chapter 6 drops the wavefront. It labels runs, unbroken spans of red in a row, with a union-find: connected-component labeling, not a search.

It is a strong yardstick. On the test image it ties with chapter 8 once both read the image with the same kernel (0.982), and on a solid 64 Mpx square it wins, 2.252 ms against chapter 7's 3.107 ms. See chapter 6's own story.

How the numbers were measured
  • One machine: an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 Laptop GPU with 24 SMs and an Intel Core i9-13900H, under WSL2 with 16 CPU threads.
  • Correctness comes first. Every kernel is checked against a CPU reference built with Numba @njit. The tests compare depth maps or label maps, not pixel counts.
  • The reference has an outside anchor. It is checked against OpenCV's connectedComponents, and against a BFS written as whole-array dilations.
  • GPU times are kernel times, with the input already on the GPU. Copies and allocation are not included, unless a line says "with the copies" or "end to end".
  • Chapters 1 to 5 time the launch and synchronize on the host. Chapters 6 to 9 and the library session time their GPU sides with CUDA events, after a clock warm-up.
  • The two benchmark images are in the repository, with their checksums.
  • Results drift between sessions on this laptop: chapter 5 on input_blobs.png read 51.03 ms in one session and 58.51 ms in another. So every comparison pairs numbers from one session where it can.
  • Every result is a committed JSON file under src/flood_fill_cuda/results/.
Related work

Breadth-first search on the GPU goes back to Harish and Narayanan (2007). Merrill, Garland and Grimshaw (2012) show how much redundant work a 2D lattice causes without duplicate culling. That is why chapters 1 to 5 claim a pixel before they enqueue it.

The enqueue in chapters 2 to 5, and in chapter 1's spill variant, uses warp-aggregated atomics: one atomic per warp instead of one per thread. The persistent kernels of chapters 2 to 5 wait on Numba's cooperative groups grid barrier.

Connected-component labeling on the GPU has a long line of work: Hawick, Leist and Playne (2010), Stava and Benes (2011), and Playne and Hawick (2018). The block-based BUF and BKE algorithms of Allegretti, Bolelli and Grana (2020) belong to it too. Chapter 5's union-find follows this line.

The library baselines are OpenCV's connectedComponents and CuPy's cupyx.scipy.ndimage.label.

Labeling runs instead of pixels is a classic CPU idea, described by He, Chao and Suzuki (2008). Chapter 6 applies it on the GPU.

Chapter 6 skips the decoupled look-back scan of Merrill and Garland (2016). There are too few rows for a multi-block scan to pay.

Every kernel is written in Python with Numba for CUDA GPUs.

Every table, every loss and every session is in the long version of this page.
